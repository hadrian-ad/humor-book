Nutrients

Where do books come from? One common metaphor is to waterways: books have "sources" that flow into the pool of the book.

There's something alchemical about the idea: one vial combines with another, and the result is something new that came from the ingredients.

If you believe, as I do, that books come from people more than come from their ingredients, you might prefer other metaphors—like in some Islamic tradition, where the metaphor isn't waterways but nutrients. A book grows from the soil of a life.

I'd like to share some of the nutrients that went into this book. You may wonder what I found humorous or inspiring or important! Things that created the conditions that this book grew out of.

For this book, I did no literature review of humor or analogous terms, which was outside the scope and purpose of this project. My goal was to kindle something in the reader, not relate this book to other books.

Poetry

D.H. Lawrence, whose poetic work often focuses on the meaning of genuine lifeness.

As we live, we are transmitters of life.
And when we fail to transmit life, life fails to flow through us.

And if, as we work, we can transmit life into our work,
life, still more life, rushes into us to compensate, to be ready
and we ripple with life through the day.

Give and it shall be given until you
is still the truth about life.
But giving life is not so easy.
It doesn’t mean handing it out to some mean fool, or letting the living dead eat you up.
It means kindling the life-quality where it was not.

Rumi, whose religious practice involved a kind of ecstatic humor, conveyed through particularly beautiful metaphors and inversions.

Anne Carson, whose Eros, The Bittersweet (1986) helps lay a central, invisible conflict in our life: writing versus speaking.

To control the boundaries is to possess oneself. For individuals to whom self-possession has become important, the influx of a sudden, strong emotion from without cannot be an unalarming event, as it may be in an oral environment where such incursions are the normal conductors of the most important information that a person receives.

Thomas Hardy, whose poems often strike me as smiling in some way. I adapted the title of his final volume of poetry (Winter in Various Moods and Meters) for this work.

These are brand new birds of twelvemonths’ growing,
Which a year ago, or less than twain,
No finches were, nor nightingales,
Nor thrushes,
But only particles of grain,
And earth, and air, and rain.

Teaching

Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain (1979) by Betty Edwards, which was my first experience with effective book-based instruction that produced tangible outcomes.

The Cartoon History of the Universe series by Larry Gonick, whose later entries are a gold standard for the use of fitted humor to alter framings. Gonick's bibliography also made the simple improvement of a brief authorial reflection.

Lying: A critical Analysis (1987) by Warren Shibles. A beautiful, unexpected look at lying—including the perspective of education as coming to believe something we initially think is a lie.

There are very few books about the lie. In this respect it will be an adventure into the practically unknown. Once this world is entered no one will return the same as before.

Humor: A Comprehensive Reference Guide. One of the weirdest books ever put together, aimless, meaty, and incomplete—an early self-publishing attempt of the World Wide Web.

Emotions in Aesthetics (1995) by Warren Shibles. Argues that ethics and aesthetics are the same: a precise, discomforting, strangly compiled book.

When similar perspectives appear in ever new contexts, they may be thought of as unity-producing refrains, variations on a theme, the rhythm of a language. Wittgenstein wrote, A repetition makes a theme appear in a quite different light.

Poetic Meter and Poetic Form (1967), by Paul Fussel. A thoughtful, prickly critic, which taught me stable ground for poetry.

And the same principle holds for the reader: at the moment of his first apprehension of the poem he functions less as semanticist than as a more or less unwitting prosodist [rhythmist]. It is the purpose of this book to help the reader to become, as prosodist, less unwitting.

Of Man (1662) by Thomas Hobbes. Beautiful language and a precise contempt for confident, incoherent language.

Or else he will find hemselfe entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twiggs; the more he struggles, the more belimed.

Radical Mycology (2016) by Peter McCoy. A book about a "neglected megascience." Especially beautiful metaphors that relate learning and teaching to fungus: networks through our environment that mushroom when conditions are right.

Poverty in mycology relative to its potential importance suggests that it is one of the biggest information gaps—if not the biggest—in the natural sciences.

Perspective

Spaces Speak, Are you Listening? (2007) by Berry Blesser and Linda-Ruth Salter. An example of teaching something that everyone is aware of, but rarely thinks about.

Cities are so noisy that residents treasure private acoustic arenas, often at the cost of feeling isolated, lonely, and anonymous.

Rethinking Sitting (2007) by Peter Opsvik. The re-examination of common things.

The similarity between the Pharaoh’s throne and the clerk’s chair shows us how we took after upper class culture rather than trying to find good working postures when the modern chair was constructed.

Tools of Conviviality (1973) by Ivan Illich. A book about people being tools of their tools—when means are swapped with ends. Anarchists often talk about prioritizing humanness in systems, but this quality is often underdefined.

The radical monopoly of overefficient tools extracts from society the increasing and costly conditioning of clients.

Wandering on the Way: Early Taoist Tales and Parables of Chuang Tzu (400 BCE) by Zhuangzi, translated by Victor Mair. A book against ethics and formal logic, and an important book in the three thousand year conflict between the proponents and opponents of writing supremecy.

If we recognize that Yao and Chieh approved of themselves, but condemned each other, we can perceive their controlling inclinations.

Orality and Literacy The Technologizing of the Word, written by a writing supremacist, the author describes the transition into literacy while struggling to overcome the incoherence of his support of that process. The author's common view is part of our strange relation to humor: our tendancy to focus on a static joke (writing) instead of on the experience of people.

The Ultimate Heresy: The Doctrine of Biblical Inerrancy (1997) Rodger Cragun. Like the religious author above, this author connects writing supremacy (religious book inerrancy) to book religions. Unlike that author, this author strongly criticizes that perspective as damaging and incoherent.

The Phenomenon of Life an anti-mechanistic perspective on the world, whose style of presentation moved me.

Eros and Magic in the Renaissance, a scholarly book on untangling the jumbled but still coherent psychology of the Renaissance.
