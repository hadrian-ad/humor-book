A Sampling of Works

Nutrients

Where do books come from? One common metaphor is to waterways: books have "sources" that flow into the pool of the book.

There's something alchemical about this metaphor: that two things combine, and something new comes from the ingredients.

But if you believe, as I do, that books come from people more than they come from other books, you might prefer other metaphors—like from some traditions, where the metaphor isn't waterways but nutrients. A book grows from the soil of a life, soil which has taken on a composition of the books and experiences added to it.

I'd like to share some of the nutrients that went into this book. You may wonder what I found humorous or inspiring or important: things that created the conditions that this book grew out of.

For this book, I did no literature review of humor or analogous terms, which was outside the scope and purpose of this project. My goal was to kindle something in the reader, not relate this book to other books.

Instead, the books below explore something about the core of this book: the ability to focus on emotions and experiences beyond texts; the way perspectives are conveyed, and to what purpose; and on matters that relate to humor family words.

Poetry

D.H. Lawrence, whose poetic work often focuses on trying to capture genuine lifeness. He pleads and demands it of the world around him.

As we live, we are transmitters of life.
And when we fail to transmit life, life fails to flow through us.

And if, as we work, we can transmit life into our work,
life, still more life, rushes into us to compensate, to be ready
and we ripple with life through the day.

Give and it shall be given until you
is still the truth about life.
But giving life is not so easy.
It doesn’t mean handing it out to some mean fool, or letting the living dead eat you up.
It means kindling the life-quality where it was not.

Thomas Hardy, whose poems often strike me as smiling in some way. I adapted the title of his final volume of poetry (Winter in Various Moods and Meters) for this work.

These are brand new birds of twelvemonths’ growing,
Which a year ago, or less than twain,
No finches were, nor nightingales,
Nor thrushes,
But only particles of grain,
And earth, and air, and rain.

Rumi, whose religious practice involves a kind of ecstatic humor, which he conveys through particularly beautiful metaphors and inversions. (Translation below by Alan Williams.)

'I know precisely what your pain is.
I'll work some magic on you for your health.
Be joyful, be relaxed, and have no fear.
- I'll do for you what rain does for the meadow'

Anne Carson, whose Eros, The Bittersweet (1986) helps lay a central, invisible-to-most conflict in our life: writing versus speaking.

To control the boundaries is to possess oneself. For individuals to whom self-possession has become important, the influx of a sudden, strong emotion from without cannot be an unalarming event, as it may be in an oral environment where such incursions are the normal conductors of the most important information that a person receives.

Teaching

Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain (1979) by Betty Edwards. This was my first experience of learning something I thought couldn't be taught.

The Cartoon History of the Universe (1991+) series by Larry Gonick. Starting with Volume II especially, these are a gold standard for the use of fitted humor in teaching. I am also inspired by his bibliography, which included a brief authorial reflection.

Lying: A Critical Analysis (1987) by Warren Shibles. One piece of Shible's Beautiful Theory of Everything, not presented well as usual.

There are very few books about the lie. In this respect it will be an adventure into the practically unknown. Once this world is entered no one will return the same as before.

Humor: A Comprehensive Reference Guide (2010). One of the weirdest books ever assembled: aimless, meaty, and incomplete. An early self-publishing attempt of the World Wide Web.

Emotions in Aesthetics (1995) by Warren Shibles. Argues that ethics and aesthetics are the same: a precise, discomforting, strangely compiled book.

When similar perspectives appear in ever new contexts, they may be thought of as unity-producing refrains, variations on a theme, the rhythm of a language. Wittgenstein wrote, A repetition makes a theme appear in a quite different light.

Poetic Meter and Poetic Form (1967), by Paul Fussell. A thoughtful, prickly critic, this book taught me how stable ground can be found in subjects often dismissed as abstract.

And the same principle holds for the reader: at the moment of his first apprehension of the poem he functions less as semanticist than as a more or less unwitting prosodist [rhythmist]. It is the purpose of this book to help the reader to become, as prosodist, less unwitting.

Of Man (1662) by Thomas Hobbes. Confident contempt for incoherence and arrogance, and the premise that we can study others by studying ourselves.

Or else he will find hemselfe entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twiggs; the more he struggles, the more belimed.

Radical Mycology (2016) by Peter McCoy. A book about a "neglected megascience," which is my perspective on joy. Contains especially beautiful metaphors, which relate learning and teaching to fungus: we network through our environment and mushroom when conditions are right, releasing spores. This book is this kind of mushroom.

Poverty in mycology relative to its potential importance suggests that it is one of the biggest information gaps—if not the biggest—in the natural sciences.

Black Athena, Volumes I-III (1987+), by Martin Bernal. A book about independence, how we think, and where conclusions come from.

Thus one encounters the paradoxical situation that while amateurs are usually unable to help scholarly advance within a model or paradigm, they are often the best people to challenge it.

The Phenomenon of Life (2002), by Christopher Alexander. A slow build-up to an anti-mechanistic perspective on the world. Its style of argument, if not its sentence-by-sentence writing, was beautiful to me.

The appearance of these properties is linked to the stability and robustness of the world.

Keywords (1976), Raymond Williams. A standard for competence in how we can use etymology to think coherently about the present day language.

Perspective

Spaces Speak, Are you Listening? (2007) by Berry Blesser and Linda-Ruth Salter. An example of teaching something that everyone is aware of—acoustics—but rarely thinks about.

Cities are so noisy that residents treasure private acoustic arenas, often at the cost of feeling isolated, lonely, and anonymous.

Rethinking Sitting (2007) by Peter Opsvik. Another re-examination of a common thing, animated by a wise perspective.

The similarity between the Pharaoh’s throne and the clerk’s chair shows us how we took after upper class culture rather than trying to find good working postures when the modern chair was constructed.

Disenchanted Night (1988), Wolfgang Schivelbusch. Peculiarly powerful insights from a historian of mentalities.

It turned out that for public lighting to be effective, it had to relate to the street after all – it needed to be street lighting. But the notion of lighting up whole cities or parts of cities uniformly from above proved to be an idée fixe of the sort that are produced now and then when technology offers new opportunities. If these are pursued to their logical conclusion, the result is often something that can be called technical monumentalism, in contrast to the practical applications of a particular technology.

Tools of Conviviality (1973) by Ivan Illich. A book about people being tools of their tools.

The radical monopoly of overefficient tools extracts from society the increasing and costly conditioning of clients.

Wandering on the Way: Early Taoist Tales and Parables of Chuang Tzu (400 BCE) by Zhuangzi, translated by Victor Mair. An important book in the three thousand year conflict between the proponents and opponents of writing-centric societies.

If we recognize that Yao and Chieh approved of themselves, but condemned each other, we can perceive their controlling inclinations.

Orality and Literacy the Technologizing of the Word (1982), Walter Ong. The author describes the change from interactive models to non-interactive models. Of particular interest is the author's struggle to justify his own support of extreme literacy.

The Hidden Life of TREES (2016), by Peter Wohlleben. A book about slow-moving forensics in the forest.

The Horn Book: Studies in Erotic Folklore and Bibliography (1966), by Gershon Legman. Imagine the worst person you know, writing beautifully petty paragraphs about the elimination of sex from culture and history.

Nothing has changed, except for the worse, since World War II.

The White Goddess: A Historical Grammar of Poetic Myth (1948), by Robert Graves. Constructs a beautiful view you can admire for a while.

The lapwing’s poetic meaning is ‘Disguise the secret’ and it is her extraordinary discretion which gives her the claim to sanctity.

Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2014), David Graeber. One of many books by an anarchist that try to help us think beyond cash relations.

To bring back nothing at all would be to cast oneself as an exploiter or a parasite. To bring back an exact equivalent would be to suggest that one no longer wishes to have anything to do with the neighbor.

Novels

Love & Sleep by John Crowley. The second book of the Aegypt Cycle, deep in mysteries.

He couldn’t wish she wouldn’t call, but the sound of her voice.

Quicksilver by Neal Stephenson. A book of light density and ecstatic construction.

Ratner's Star (1976) by Don DeLillo. Dark, ecstatic, and unrelenting technologic absurdism

Cyril: “’All things are water,’ said the Greek.”
Una: “’All things flow,’ said the Greeker of the two.”

The Satyricon (50) by Gaius Petronius. Two thousand years old, and still a shining example of a the absurdity of empire.

The Story of the Stone (1771) by Cao Xueqin, translated by David Hawkes. Immense and tricky humanity from imperial China.

Truth becomes fiction when the fiction’s true;
Real becomes not-real where the unreal’s real

The Foundation Pit (1938) by Andrey Platonov, translated by Robert and Elizabeth Chandler, and Alga Meerson. A critical book about the darkness of authoritarian idealism, with upsetting, rumpled imagery.

Each was existing without the least surplus of life, and during sleep only the heart remained alive to take care of the man.

The Twelve Chairs by Ilya Ilf and Evgeny Petrov, translated by Anne Fisher. Makes you laugh.

The reserves of irony Viktor Mikhailovich had stored up in ten years of revolution were inexhaustible.

If on a winter's night a traveler by Italo Calvino. Beguiling in its unexpectedness.

Men in Prison (1929) by Victor Serge. About the destruction of life in prison.

The Satanic Verses (1988), by Salman Rushdie. The best novel.

The Atom Station, (1948) by Halldor Laxness. Wind and society.

“That’s just like you northerners, to start trying to talk to people,” said the cook when I returned to the kitchen.
Rebellion stirred in me and I replied, “I am people.”

Tales of Potosi (1730), Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela, translated by Frances M. López-Morillas. A kind of insane book: entertaining, raunchy stories, told by moralist.
